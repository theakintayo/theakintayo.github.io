While I was taking a course on ‘Learning How to Learn” on Coursera, I learnt that sleep is an integral part of retaining memory, as the diffused mode of learning occurs during sleep. This is when the chunks of knowledge gathered during the focused mode are connected, allowing the brain to further understand and ingrain the knowledge.

Luckily, I had found this dataset from the Statistics course on Udacity. It has the results from a BBC test on memory where you are shown two set of 12 pictures with five minutes breaks in-between, then you take a test where you’re shown different pictures and asked if they were part of the pictures you had seen and which set you saw them in. After the test, you get a result showing your recognition memory score which refers to your ability to remember if you had seen a picture or not, and your temporal memory score which refers to your ability to remember which set you saw the picture in. Students taking the Statistics were told to enter their scores from this test and the number of hours they slept the night before.

The dataset had 4 columns and 9158 rows including the header row. The columns were “Timestamp (the time each student made the entry)”, ”How many hours did you sleep last night?”, “Recognition Score” and “Temporal Memory Score”.

Results and Discussion

a. Descriptive Statistics

There isn’t much difference in the values of the measures of central tendency (mean, median, mode) and measures of dispersion (standard deviation, variance). However, the range is now 15.0 due to the rows removed. Also the percentiles show the values for people below each percentile, 10% of people sleep for 4.5 hours, have a recognition score of 75% and temporal memory score of 63%.

b. Percentiles

This shows that 5% of people sleep for 0 hours, have 68% and 57% recognition and temporal scores and 95% people sleep for a maximum of 9.5 hours, with 100% and 95% recognition and temporal scores. These shows that more people have higher recognition score compared to temporal memory scores. Also if your temporal memory score is above 95%, you’re in the top 5% as opposed to recognition score where even a 100% recognition score putting you in the top 25%.

c. Correlations

This is consistent with the finding that memory scores are positively correlated with the hours of sleep with temporal memory scores being more highly correlated. This also shows that these correlation is significant at an alpha level of 0.01 with the correlation between temporal memory scores and hours of sleep and recognition score is significant even at an alpha level of 0.001 based on the significance values from the table.

d. Independent Samples T-Test

This shows that at 0.05, 0.01 and even 0.001 alpha level there’s a significant difference between the means of the below recommended and recommended groups for the temporal memory scores judging by the p-values. However, the difference in the means of recognition scores of the sleep groups is significant only at an alpha level of 0.05.

The positive values of Cohen’s d (0.058 and 0.091) indicate that the post-treatment (recommended hours of sleep group) has higher means compared to the pre-treatment (below recommended hours of sleep) for both the recognition score and temporal memory score respectively.

e. Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)

This shows that at a 0.05 and 0.01 alpha level, there is a significant difference in the temporal memory scores depending on the sleep group judging by the significance of .002. Whilst, there’s no significant difference in recognition memory scores at both 0.05 and 0.01 alpha level. This concludes that sleep plays a crucial role in temporal memory which might not necessarily be true for recognition memory score.

Eta-squared also called explanation refers to the proportion of total variation that is due to between-group differences. So, from the table we can infer that only 0.1% of recognition and temporal memory scores can be explained due to the differences in sleep groups. 99.9% remains unexplained.

f. Tukey’s HSD

According to what I learnt from the statistics course, we only use Tukey’s HSD when we have the same sample size for each group, but I wanted to see the representation of results. Also, SPSS gave a disclaimer regarding the group sizes being unequal and Type 1 error (rejecting a null hypothesis when it is actually true) levels not guaranteed.

This shows that only the mean difference between the groups below the recommended sleep hours and those within the recommended hours is significant for temporal memory scores, this interprets as that the two groups are honestly significantly different.

Conclusion

Based on these results we reject the null hypothesis and accept the alternative hypothesis for the relationship between the hours of sleep and the temporal memory scores. However, there is not enough evidence to reject the null hypothesis between the hours of sleep and recognition memory scores, so we fail to reject the null hypothesis. Therefore, it can be concluded that the number of hours you sleep, i.e. sleeping for the recommended duration has a positive effect on your temporal memory, i.e. your ability to remember where and when you saw something. The effect of the hours of sleep on the recognition score could not be proven.
