Your Water is Clear doesn’t mean it’s Clean

There are different types of contamination and different levels to contamination. We have the physical, microbiological and chemical contamination.

The physical contamination has to do with color, turbidity- (the amount of suspended particles in your water, say sand, clay, dirts, etc) and taste. The microbiological contamination which is the most paramount concern of a water scientist, refers to the presence of bacteria, protozoa, viruses and helminths. The most uncommon form of contamination is the chemical contamination which is the presence of chemicals like arsenic, copper, mercury in the water. This is mostly due to industrial processes and effluent in the water.

The physical contamination is usually the easiest to deal with and is responsible for the “dirty” look of your water. The muddiness is usually a product of turbidity which can be reduced through sedimentation, that is allowing the water to settle for a certain period of time — natural settlement, or using natural coagulants like moringa seed powder in the water, thoroughly mixing and then settling for a while, or the use of chemical coagulants to speed up the process of coagulation-particles coming together/merging to form bigger particles.

After the process of sedimentation, the water can then be transferred into another bucket or container. You can further treat your water by filtering the water produced from sedimentation. In households, this could be done using cloths especially cotton materials, folding them 4 times to have 16 layers and then placing the cloth on a bucket and sieving the water through the cloth. You can also use bio-sand filters, ceramic candle filters or ceramic filters.

After this process, a large proportion of turbidity is gone, which means your water is now clear. However, this doesn’t mean the water is safe to drink yet.

There are still chances of microbial contamination which are responsible for cholera, diarrhea and other forms of waterborne diseases.

You can treat your water further by disinfecting it, there are several methods to do this at home. You can either use the most popular method of chemical disinfection, Chlorine. You just have to make sure to read the directions on usage and dose which is how many drops or quantity per litres of water depending on whether you bought powdered or liquid form. Some form of microorganisms can however escape this treatment due to the presence of a protective shield called cysts.

Or you can use the most popular traditional method of disinfection- boiling. Most microorganisms cannot survive past the boiling temperature of 100 degrees celsius, which is the point your water starts bubbling. Although, the boiling point depends on the altitude, that is, your position in relation to the sea level, a 100 degrees celsius is safe enough to treat the water. You can then allow the water to cool before transferring to a sealed container with taps to avoid recontamination.

Talking about recontamination, a safe way to avoid this after disinfection is to use the SODIS (Solar Disinfection) method. This means storing your water in PET bottles, and placing them outside on a flat surface for the sun to heat them up and kill or inactivate the microorganisms in your water. Asides the heat, this process makes use of the UV rays from the sun to disinfect your water. However, the duration of the exposure to sun is dependent on the weather, whether it’s sunny or cloudy.

It is free from the risk of recontamination because the water can be drunk directly from the PET bottles. Also, researches have shown that there’s no chemical contamination from exposing the PET bottles to sunlight, so, yes, the water is safe to drink.

Side note: Do not store your water in transparent containers, as this supports the growth of algal blooms in your water as it allows the rays of sunlight to pass through it.
