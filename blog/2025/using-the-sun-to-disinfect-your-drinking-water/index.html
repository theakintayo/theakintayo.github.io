There are different ways of disinfecting(inactivating pathogens) water, chemical and ultraviolet radiation. The disinfection of water through ultraviolet radiation can be artificial radiation which needs electricity and lamps to generate energy or natural energy which uses the sun’s energy for solar disinfection.

Ultraviolet radiation is a type of energy along the electromagnetic spectrum, where different kinds of radiation are defined by their wavelength. Ultraviolet has wavelength in the range of 100–400 nanometer with visible light in the 400–700 nanometer range.

Ranges of UV radiation

· Vacuum UV (Only exists in the vacuum): 100 -200 nm

· UV-C (Deep Ultraviolet): 200–280nm

· UV-B: 280 -315 nm

· UV-A: 315 -400nm

Note: The germicidal peak of UV radiation is at 260nm which is in the UV-C range, hence why most commercial UV systems target this wavelength of 260nm. UV-C is the most effective against bacteria then, protozoa cysts, then, viruses and bacterial spores which need larger dose to inactivate those kinds of pathogens.

A dosage of UV radiation is the product of Intensity of UV radiation * Time.

The typical dose is 400J/m2 at which there would more than 4 log removal of bacteria, almost 4 log removal of rotavirus, but only a little bit more than 1 log removal of Bacillus subtilis spores with e-coli readily removed with even greater log removal than bacteria.

Log Reduction Value is a performance parameter that refers to the number of log units by which a chemical or other contaminants is reduced through treatment.

For example, if a particular sample has 1,000,000 counts of contaminants, a reduction to 100,000 which is a 90% reduction is equivalent to 1 LRV. If it is a 99% reduction, that is the concentration of contaminant is reduced to 10,000 count, the log removal is 2.

Therefore, 4 log removal of bacteria can be visualized as reducing a 100,000 count of bacteria to 10.

UV-C is quite effective in water treatment, has no impact on pH. However, turbidity could shield particles and a higher dose is required at low temperatures. UV-C can be generated using lamps which requires electricity, e.g. low-pressure mercury lamps (with 254nm), medium-pressure lamps (200–440nm) and LED lamps at a particular wavelength at a lower cost than mercury lamps. However this is mostly used in medium-high income countries, for low-income countries, a cost-effective alternative is the solar disinfection.

Solar Disinfection (SODIS)

Solar Disinfection (SODIS) works by simply taking a clean plastic (PET) bottle, filling it with water and then lying it on a flat surface, maybe a rooftop and exposing it to natural sunlight for at least 6 hours, or if it is a cloudy day, for two days. After this exposure, the water is safe to drink. Over 20 countries, 5 million people use this method as a disinfection process for their water treatment.

SODIS doesn’t use UV-C radiation because as the sunlight passes through the atmosphere, all of the UV-C and even about 90% of UV-B is absorbed by the ozone, water vapor, oxygen and carbon dioxide in the atmosphere, but UV-A is less affected by the atmosphere. Therefore, the UV-radiation reaching the earth’s surface is largely composed of UV-A and a bit of UV-B component. So, SODIS works differently from UV-C disinfection and it does so with three main mechanisms.

1. Direct Inactivation: UV-B radiation can still directly inactivate cells. It won’t be much absorbed by the DNA but other components within the cells, like proteins, enzymes can be affected. However, much of the UV-B is removed by PET bottles, so this is just a minor effect.

2. Indirect Inactivation: The UV-A radiation is absorbed by some intermediate species which go on to create reactive oxygen species like hydroxyl radical or hydrogen peroxide, which causes damage to the microbes. This could be internal with these reactive oxygen species (ROS) been generated inside the cell if the cell contains chromophores, which are typically present in bacterial cells or externally through dissolved organic matter or dissolved ion.

3. Heat: If the temperature gets to 60–70oc, it gets to a point of pasteurization, but even at 50oc, heat plays an important role in the effects of radiation.

Factors Influencing the Effect of SODIS

· UV-radiation: The height of sun is important, because the higher the sun, the higher the radiation level and this varies with the time of the day and year.

· Latitude and Altitude: Radiation doesn’t fall equally on the earth and the higher the altitude, the more the radiation with UV levels increasing by about 10% for every 1000 metres.

· Ozone and Ground Reflection: The ozone in the atmosphere reduces UV and can vary spacially, varies over the year, and even across the year.

· Water Column: The depth of the water in the bottle is also important as UV radiation is attenuated (depreciated) by water. Therefore, if the bottle is too large and the water column were too thick, then the UV wouldn’t penetrate to the centre of the bottle.

· Characteristics of Water:

a. Temperature: When water temperature reaches 50oc, there’s a synergy and only about one hour of UV exposure is needed.

b. Turbidity (Measure of suspended particles in water): This negatively affects SODIS and if the turbidity is >30NTU, it is advised to pretreat the water with filtration (passing the water through a clean cloth), or coagulation (Using coagulants such as alum and mixing thoroughly to accumulate particles) and sedimentation (allowing particles to settle before sieving).

Efficacy of SODIS

Protozoa such as Giardia cysts, Cryptosporidium spores are inactivated: 2->4 LRV

Bacteria (3->5 LRV): Salmonella have been found to be more persistent than E.coli and Shigella and Vibrio Cholera are relatively susceptible to ultraviolet radiation.

Viruses (1->5 LRV): Adenovirus is quite resistant to SODIS but also to UV-C radiation, whilst echovirus is relatively easier to disinfect with SODIS.

Concerns Raised About the Exposure Plastic Materials to the Sun for Water Treatment

· Antimony: A chemical used as catalyst in the production of PET, but it only leaches into water at appreciable levels when the water is in contact with the plastic for many weeks or longer or if the temperature exceeds 70oc. However, this doesn’t happen during SODIS treatment since the water is only in the bottle for few days and temperature rarely exceeds 50oc.

· Adipates and Phthalates: These are used as softeners in the production of certain plastics like PVC used in plastic pipes. Although, not particularly toxic, the softeners can present a threat to health if they are consumed in large quantities. Thankfully, these softeners are not used in the production of PET which are used in SODIS, so they can’t leach into the water.

· Aldehydes: These are formed when the plastic is heated in the manufacturing process for PET bottles, however, laboratory investigations have found that the exposure to sun has no effect on the concentrations of aldehydes that could be measured especially for acetaldehydes. However, the concentration of formaldehydes does increase with the length of exposure, but the concentrations remain far below the standard regulatory limit for drinking water. Formaldehydes and Acetaldehydes can be a problem for bottled carbonated water because the higher pressure and lower pH within these bottles can result in aldehydes in stored water. However, the pressure and pH are moderate in SODIS treatment, so these chemicals are not found at levels of concern in SODIS treated water.

· Bisphenol A: A chemical present in some plastic bottles which is suspected to cause breast cancer. However, this is only an issue with polycarbonate bottles and plastics and not in PET bottles used for SODIS.

· Genotoxicity: Mutations in certain plants or protozoa or bacteria cells as a result of exposure to water. Studies have shown that water stored in plastic bottles for long periods like several months, cell mutations could be observed. However, this occurred with or without exposure to UV radiation, so, while it might be an issue for long term storage, it is not directly pertinent to SODIS.

Since SODIS involves neither long storage time nor high temperatures, there has not been any evidence that SODIS introduces any harm or chemicals to treated water.

Advantages of SODIS

· Highly effective, although UV-C is more effective especially against viruses and protozoa.

· The SODIS is inexpensive and simple to operate

· There’s low risk of recontamination with SODIS because the water is typically drunk from the same container where it has been treated.

Challenges of SODIS

· Both UV-C and SODIS are limited to low turbidity waters because particles can shield pathogens from the radiation.

· UV-C requires electricity to run the lamps.

· SODIS is highly dependent on the climate, thus takes significant time.

· Only small volumes of water can be treated at a time in a batch of SODIS treatment.
